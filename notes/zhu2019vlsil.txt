Title: Indoor Topological Localization Using a Visual Landmark Sequence
Author(s): Jiasong Zhu, Qing Li, Rui Cao, Ke Sun, Tao Liu, Jonathan M. Garibaldi, Qingquan Li, Bozhi Liu, Guoping Qiu (Shenzhen University, University of Nottingham, Henan University of Economics and Law)
Group: Landmark / text / object recognition
Published: Remote Sensing 11(1), 73, 2019 (open access, CC BY). Journal extension of their GeoAI 2017 workshop paper.


WHAT IT DOES
I read the whole paper. The method (VLSIL) localises a walking person from phone video without any image database. Landmarks are ordinary fixed objects: office doors (door plus doorplate), fire extinguishers, stairs, elevators, toilet tags and corridor corners/intersections. Video frames are sampled at about 3 fps, selective search proposes 300 patches per frame, and a fine-tuned AlexNet (only the output layer is retrained, 10 classes including "wall/background") labels them. Training used about 1,300 images, partly from the internet so it can work in a new building without retraining. The detected landmark types, in the order they appear, are matched against a topological map (a directed graph built by hand from the floor plan) with a second-order hidden Markov model and an extended Viterbi algorithm. Second order means the walking direction is included, which removes a lot of ambiguity. A multiple-object landmark that is only partly seen is handled with a probability matrix.
Experiments: 1.9 hours of video from 5 volunteers with 3 phones, in two Nottingham office buildings (54 and 65 landmarks), 7 routes, phone worn on the upper arm looking sideways. Object recognition accuracy was 98.6% overall (ANN on Gist features 81.8%, SVM 44.3%), and all landmarks on all routes were detected. With a known start, all 7 routes were localised. With an unknown start, 5 routes ended with a single candidate path and 2 routes still had two candidates. The plain HMM failed to localise without a known start (for Route 6, 18,346 possible paths vs. 2 for the second-order model). Route 1 was localised after 6 landmarks and Route 7 after 9 (unknown start), or after 3 with a known start.


RELEVANT TO MY WORK
It answers the "every corridor looks the same" problem that breaks single-image methods: one landmark is ambiguous, but the order of landmarks along a route is not. It needs no photo database, only the floor plan and a landmark detector, and it treats corners/intersections as a landmark class, close to my "unique corner" idea. A known start (for example from a QR code or a sign) helps a lot, which suggests a hybrid with the sign-reading papers. Because the model includes walking direction, it also gives me a direction of travel, which an arrow needs.


USE IT FOR
- Disambiguating similar places with sequences instead of single images.
- Landmark classes that already exist in buildings (doors, extinguishers, stairs, lifts, toilets) and a detector trained once for all buildings.
- Building the topological map from a floor plan, and the HMM2/Viterbi matching.
- Experiment design: number of landmarks needed to localise, effect of known vs. unknown start, comparison against a plain HMM.


CAVEATS
- Topological localisation only: the output is a node on the map, not a metric position or heading, so I would still need SLAM/ARCore for the arrow between nodes.
- Not real time: about 7 s per landmark image (3-4 s selective search plus about 3 s for 300 patches through the CNN); the authors say newer detectors could fix this. Matching itself is fast (0.043 s per route).
- Needs several landmarks before it is certain (6 to 9 in the harder building with an unknown start), and a gap in the video breaks the sequence.
- The topological map is built by hand, side-view capture on the upper arm is unusual, and only office corridors in two buildings were tested. AlexNet is an old detector.
- Doorplate text was not used because of motion blur.
